Strategic Diagnosis

Case framing & formulation of the central strategic question

1.1 Market Breakthrough

Strategic diagnosis

Transforming the surplus-food market

  • Shift from an unmonetized operational cost (waste & spoilage) to a structured, monetized market.
  • The innovative Surprise Bag concept reduces SKU management burden for retailers.
  • Merchants turn end-of-day inventory from a liability into incremental revenue and foot traffic.
Core insight: TGTG does not “create” food—it reallocates surplus that would otherwise be discarded, at near-zero marginal cost for partners.

1.2 Central Strategic Question

Strategic diagnosis

Causal strategic question

How can Too Good To Go use horizontal and vertical integration to consolidate the network effects of its two-sided platform—while securing financial profitability in the face of disintermediation risks and pressure from large grocery incumbents?

Key tensions embedded in the question

  • Scale vs. control: geographic and partner expansion without eroding platform stickiness.
  • Growth vs. margin: subsidizing demand while monetizing supply sustainably.
  • Platform vs. pipeline: avoiding bypass as partners and consumers learn the model.

Two-Sided Platform Economics

Pricing asymmetry, cross-subsidy, and network externalities

2.1 Pricing Asymmetry & Cross-Subsidy

Platform economics
125M Registered consumers (demand side)
175k Partner stores (supply side)

Demand side — consumers

  • Free access (€0)
  • Low entry barriers to maximize user density
  • Cross-subsidized by merchant fees

Supply side — merchants

  • Fixed annual subscription: €89
  • Variable commission: €1.79 per bag
  • Surplus is marginal-cost-neutral or negative for partners

Economic rationale for merchants

  • Incremental revenue and in-store traffic
  • 61% new customers; 41% make additional purchases

2.2 Network Effects & Platform Dynamics

Platform economics

Indirect network externalities (local density)

  • More users in a geography → more attractive for stores to list bags.
  • More stores → more choice and convenience → more users.
  • Local density matters: value is geo-specific, not purely global.

Switching costs

  • Habit, favorites, and saved payment flow on the app
  • Reputation & ratings on the supply side
  • Operational integration for large chains

Multi-homing & disintermediation risk

  • Consumers may use several surplus-food apps
  • Merchants can list on competing platforms
  • Large retailers may internalize surplus sales

Competitive Positioning

Willingness to pay vs. cost dynamics — “Quick Meal” or something else?

3.1 Value Trade-offs & Cost Structure

Competitive positioning

Does TGTG hold an advantage in “Quick Meal”?

Consumer trade-offs (costs)

  • Lower predictability (mystery bag)
  • Time constraint (pickup before closing)
  • No delivery — customer bears pickup cost

Consumer trade-offs (benefits / WTP)

  • Deep discount (~70% off typical price)
  • Eco-responsible consumption narrative
  • Discovery & variety at low price

Ultra-light cost structure (TGTG)

  • Zero COGS (food owned by partners)
  • Zero kitchen / preparation costs
  • Zero delivery cost (100% customer pickup)

3.2 Positioning Diagnosis

Competitive positioning
TGTG is not a direct competitor to quick-service restaurants or delivery aggregators (Uber Eats, Deliveroo).

Strategic role in the value chain

  • Complementor to the food retail & hospitality chain—not a substitute for planned meals.
  • Occupies a dual niche: surplus recovery for partners + discounted, mission-driven meals for consumers.
  • Competes indirectly for “occasion” spend and attention, not for standard lunch/delivery occasions.

Scope of the Firm

Horizontal & vertical integration — core strategic topic

4.1 Horizontal Integration

Scope of the firm

Scale & scope expansion

  • Geographic scaling: international presence in 19 countries (Europe & North America) to reach critical mass.
  • Partner segment diversification: from independent merchants (~119k) toward large grocery & hospitality accounts (~85k outlets).

Illustrative large-account partners

  • Grocery: Carrefour, Aldi, Whole Foods
  • Hospitality: Accor and similar chains

Horizontal moves widen the network, improve local density, and raise barriers for smaller rival platforms.

Scope of the firm

Downstream / B2B SaaS — TGTG Platforms

  • Acquisition of CodaBene

Technological integration

Non-Market Strategy & ESG

Regulatory leverage and impact-aligned business model

5.1 Regulatory & Institutional Leverage

Non-market strategy

Binding legislative frameworks

  • France: Loi Garot and related obligations on food waste
  • EU directives setting quantitative waste-reduction targets
  • Rising compliance costs for retailers → demand for turnkey solutions

Strategic implication

  • Regulation shifts surplus from “optional CSR” to operational necessity.
  • TGTG + TGTG Platforms positioned as compliance and reporting partners—not only a consumer app.

Operational Recommendations

Actionable strategic roadmap

6. Strategic Roadmap — Three Axes

Recommendations
Axis 1

Vertical integration — B2B SaaS

Monetize TGTG Platforms (ex-CodaBene) via recurring SaaS subscriptions with major grocery chains—moving from marketplace fees alone to embedded software revenue.

Axis 2

Horizontal integration — North America

Accelerate U.S./Canada penetration through framework agreements with regional supermarket chains, replicating European large-account playbook at scale.

Axis 3

Platform optimization

Roll out algorithmic dynamic pricing broadly to maximize bag sell-through during store-closing windows and improve unit economics per partner.
