1. Case framing & strategic problem formulation

§1Strategic diagnosis

Strategic diagnosis: from industry context to a causal strategic question.

1.1 Market breakthrough — surplus food

  • Shift from unvalued operational cost (waste, disposal, write-offs) to a structured, monetized market.
  • The Surprise Bag concept reduces SKU-level management burden for merchants while turning end-of-day inventory into revenue.
  • Category creation: food surplus moves from compliance cost to growth and traffic driver.

Why this matters strategically

  • TGTG sits at the intersection of market design (platform) and scope choices (integration).
  • Success depends on density, partner mix, and defensibility against bypass and large retailers.
  • Financial sustainability (break-even 2023) must coexist with mission-driven growth.

Demand side (consumers)

  • Free access — zero monetary barrier to adoption.
  • Goal: maximize user density and local liquidity of surplus bags.
  • Consumers subsidized by merchant-side fees (classic two-sided model).

Supply side (merchants)

  • Combined pricing: fixed annual subscription (~€89) + variable commission (~€1.79 per bag).
  • Surplus inventory is often zero or negative marginal cost for the merchant.
  • TGTG delivers incremental revenue and in-store traffic: 61% new customers, 41% add-on purchases.

2.2 Network externalities & platform dynamics

§2Platform economics

Indirect network effects

More consumers → higher sell-through for merchants → more attractive partner base → richer choice for consumers. Effects are primarily local (geo-density).

Switching costs

Habit, saved favorites, trust in quality/safety, and operational integration (staff workflows, pickup windows) raise switching costs for both sides — modest but growing with B2B tools.

Multi-homing risk

Merchants and consumers can use parallel apps or informal channels. Large retailers may internalize surplus sales — core disintermediation threat.

Strategic implication: horizontal scale (coverage) and vertical integration (software, data, upstream supply) reinforce the same objective — make TGTG the default infrastructure for surplus food, not merely a listing app.

3. Competitive positioning — Porter’s Five Forces

§3Industry structure

Industry attractiveness for surplus-food marketplaces: low direct rivalry and buyer power, offset by a high substitute threat (meals, delivery, in-store markdowns).

Threat of new entrants

Moderate
  • Low app & marketplace coding barriers
  • High network & scale walls (local density)

Bargaining power of suppliers (stores)

Low–moderate
  • Independents: low power
  • Retail & hotel chains: moderate power

Rivalry among existing competitors

Low
  • First-mover dominance in category
  • Category leader across 19 countries

Bargaining power of buyers (users)

Low
  • Highly fragmented demand
  • Zero signup costs

Threat of substitutes

High
  • QSRs & fast casual
  • Delivery (Uber Eats, Deliveroo)
  • In-store markdowns & waste routes

Strategic read-through: substitutes define the WTP ceiling; integration and network scale defend against entrant and chain bypass.

4. Scope of the firm — vertical & technological integration

§4Core topic

4.2 Upstream, downstream & tech integration

Downstream / B2B SaaS

TGTG Platforms (incl. CodaBene acquisition): embed expiry-date and in-store surplus management in supermarket IT.

Shift: reactive resale → proactive store-level waste prevention.

Upstream — TGTG Parcels

Direct capture of FMCG factory overstock before it enters retail channels — expands supply beyond store closing hours.

Technological integration

In-house control of core IT; deployment of dynamic pricing algorithms to optimize clearance at closing time.

Vertical moves reduce reliance on pure marketplace fees, increase switching costs for enterprise partners, and address disintermediation by owning the operational layer — not just the consumer interface.

5. Non-market strategy & ESG alignment

§5Institutional context

5.1 Regulatory leverage

  • Exploit binding frameworks: e.g. France’s Garot law, EU directives on food-waste reduction targets.
  • Regulation turns waste from a private externality into a compliance and reputational priority for retailers — increasing WTP for TGTG solutions.
  • Non-market strategy complements platform growth (lobbying, standards, B2B compliance tooling).

5.2 Impact business model (B-Corp)

  • 450M meals saved; 1.34M tonnes CO₂ avoided (impact metrics cited in plan).
  • Alignment of social/environmental value with financial viability — break-even reached in 2023.
  • ESG credibility supports enterprise sales and consumer trust; must stay consistent with pricing and partner practices.

§6Actionable roadmap
Axis 1
Vertical B2B

Monetize TGTG Platforms (ex-CodaBene) as recurring SaaS

Package expiry and surplus-management software for grocery giants under subscription contracts — diversify revenue beyond per-bag commissions and deepen enterprise lock-in.

Axis 2
Horizontal

Accelerate North American penetration via retail frameworks

Sign master agreements with regional supermarket chains to achieve local density quickly and preempt copycat or internal marketplace solutions.

Axis 3
Platform

Roll out algorithmic dynamic pricing

Generalize dynamic pricing at store-closing windows to maximize bag sell-through, balance supply–demand spikes, and improve unit economics for merchants and TGTG.
